# Assignment 02: Synthesis Blueprint

## 1. Audience Profile

My audience is college students or can also gear towards anyone in the education system who already use AI tools like ChatGPT but haven't been taught how to use them well. Most have typed a homework question into a chatbot or used it to clean up a paragraph before turning something in.They already know the basics of how to open AI tools like ChatGPT and type in prompts. What they don't know is where the line is between using AI to help them think and using it to skip thinking altogether. They also don't think much about what happens to the information they type in, or how AI shows up outside of school.

What they need is something clear and straight forward. But also, something that is not out of touch of what AI is actually good for.But also, how do we prevent ourselves from getting hurt by it. They're not looking to read a whole book on AI ethics. They want quick, usable answers for the specific situation they're in so things like, writing a paper, building a resume, or figuring out if something online is real, so they can get an easy answer and move on.

---

## 2. Format Choice

I'm creating a practical guide organized by situation not by AI tools. THey can look up things like "can I use AI on this paper?" or "should I let AI write my resume?" or even "Can you prep me for this test or interview?" Organizing what they're going through at the moment makes the guide findable, and each section can stand alone so nobody has to read the whole thing to get an answer.

---

## 3. Annotated Outline

### 1. Start Here: What is AI Actually Good At?

This sets the foundation for everything else. It explains what generative AI does well, what is wrong with it, and why "just because it sounds confident" doesn't mean "it's correct." Also covers how common AI already is in everyday life, so students understand this isn't a niche tool they can opt out of.

**Sources:** UNESCO, "Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research" (Source 1); Pew Research Center, "Artificial Intelligence in Daily Life" (Source 13)

### 2. Using AI for School Without Cheating Yourself

This is the academic integrity section. It covers the difference between AI that supports your thinking and AI that replaces it, why the second one costs you even when you don't get caught, and how to check your own use. It also gives us a reality check on if you removed the AI, could you still explain this?

**Sources:** Teaching@Tufts, "How is AI Helping (or Hindering) Learning?" (Source 3); Monash University, "Responsible and Ethical Use of AI" (Source 15)

### 3. Writing and Studying: Where AI Actually Helps

The most practical section. It Walks through the writing process like brainstorming, outlining, drafting, revising pretty my the whole schooling aspect, and shows where AI fits at each stage and where it should stay out. Also talks about studying: "generating practice questions, explaining a confusing concept a second way, summarizing your own notes rather than the reading you skipped."

**Sources:** Nathan Jung, *The Process of Generative AI Writing: A Practical Guide for Undergraduates* (Source 5); American Psychological Association, "How Teaching and Learning Will Change in the Age of AI" (Source 4)

### 4. Resumes, Applications, and Interviews

This is a big shift from school to job hunting. It covers the right sequences when transitioning from school to career jobs where, you write your own material first, then use AI for feedback, plus using AI to prep for interviews, and also research employers. Includes what not to paste into a AI during a job search, and a reminder that anything on your resume has to be something you can actually defend in the room.

**Sources:** Brown University Career Center, "Using AI in Your Job Search" (Source 7); University of Tennessee, "AI in the Job Search" (Source 11)

### 5. Staying Safe Against AI: Your Information, Your Voice

This would be the safety section. It covers what happens to information you type into any of the AI tools, how AI scams work like, voice cloning, deepfake video, fake job offers targeting students, and how to authenticate something before you act on it. It also touches on AI in high-stakes areas like health, where the rule is simple: "AI is not your doctor, and a human expert still has to be in the loop."

**Sources:** Federal Trade Commission, "AI Impersonation Protections" (Source 12); World Health Organization, "Artificial Intelligence for Health" (Source 14)
